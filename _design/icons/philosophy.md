# Torque Line

A single stroke, pulled taut, carries the whole weight of meaning. Torque Line is a discipline of drawing in which every form is built from one continuous gauge of line, set on a strict twenty-four unit field, and nothing is permitted that the line cannot hold. There is no fill to hide behind and no shading to soften a decision. The mark either stands, balanced and legible at the size of a fingertip, or it is redrawn. Every glyph should read as meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise and countless quiet corrections that no viewer will ever see.

Space is the second material. Each form keeps a two-unit margin of air inside its field, and the negative space inside a shape is measured as carefully as the shape itself: the hole of a rotor, the gap between two wheels, the breath inside a heart. Forms are drawn from a small vocabulary — the circle, the right angle, the gentle radius of a turned corner — and these parts recur across the family so that every member feels forged in the same workshop. Painstaking attention to optical weight means a circle is allowed to run fractionally wider than a square, so both feel equal to the eye.

Color is rationed. Machined black does the work; one signal red is reserved for the moment that matters — the active state, the warning, the thing to press. Red is never decoration. When it appears, it should feel like a tail-light in fog: rare, exact and impossible to miss. Around the marks, the ground stays pale and quiet, a workbench wiped clean before precise work.

Rhythm comes from repetition. Laid out in rows, the family should read like a set of instruments in a fitted tray — identical in weight, varied in silhouette, each in its own recess. The terminals are rounded, the joins are rounded, and every endpoint lands on the grid. The result must look like master-level execution: a system someone labored over for weeks, where any single mark could be enlarged to a wall and still hold its line.

Text is a whisper. Small mono labels and coordinates sit beneath the work like stamped part numbers — clinical, sparse, never explaining what the form already says.
